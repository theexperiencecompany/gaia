There's a specific kind of lying that happens in startup marketing: the demo account. Perfectly staged data, a calendar full of plausible-looking meetings, an inbox with exactly three unread emails. Everyone knows it's fake, including the people who made it.

So instead of a staged tour, here's the unglamorous truth about how the two of us actually use GAIA to build GAIA — what runs every day, what we turned off, and where it still falls short.

### The morning briefing replaced our standup prep

Every morning at 9, GAIA sends each of us a briefing: calendar for the day, PRs that moved overnight, emails that actually need a reply, and anything it flagged from Discord while we slept. It pulls from GitHub, Gmail, Google Calendar, and Slack — the same [integrations](/marketplace) everyone else gets.

The honest impact isn't the five minutes it saves. It's that we stopped *checking things to see if anything happened*. That loop — open GitHub, open inbox, open calendar, scan, close — used to run a dozen times a day each. Now it runs when GAIA tells us something changed.

![The GAIA dashboard we look at every morning](/images/screenshots/dashboard.png)

### Todos we don't type

Neither of us maintains a todo list anymore. Not because we're organized — because we're not. GAIA creates tasks from wherever they actually originate:

- A user reports a bug in Discord → task, tagged, prioritized
- An investor email mentions "send me the metrics by Friday" → task with a Friday deadline
- A meeting ends with "Dhruv will look into the Cloudflare cold starts" → task, assigned, linked to the meeting

The task list stopped being a thing we maintain and became a thing that maintains itself. We just work it down.

![Tasks created from email, Discord, and meetings — not typed](/images/screenshots/todos.png)

### Workflows: the boring superpower

The features we personally use most aren't the impressive ones. They're the dumb, repetitive ones we described to GAIA once:

- **Weekly changelog draft** — every Friday, GAIA reads the week's merged PRs and drafts release notes. We edit maybe 20% of it.
- **Community triage** — new Discord questions get summarized and filed; the ones that look like bugs become GitHub issues with reproduction context attached.
- **Investor updates** — the monthly numbers get pulled into a draft before we even remember it's month-end.

None of these is individually remarkable. But we counted: it's roughly a full workday per week, per person, that we don't spend on this anymore. That's the "[get a workday back](/)" claim on our homepage — it started as a measurement of our own week, not a slogan.

![Workflows run on schedules and triggers — described once, in plain English](/images/screenshots/workflows.png)

### What didn't work

Honesty section. Things we tried and turned off:

- **Auto-send replies.** GAIA drafts, we send. We tried full auto-send for low-stakes email for two weeks; the one bad send wasn't worth the hundred good ones. Drafts-only is the right default and it's staying that way.
- **Pinging on every calendar change.** Early GAIA told you everything it noticed. It felt attentive for a day and exhausting for a week. Now most observations land in the morning briefing, and only genuinely urgent things interrupt.
- **Letting it near production.** GAIA files the issue; it does not touch the deploy. Some trust is earned in years, not sprints.

### Why this matters for the product

Dogfooding is a startup cliché, but for a proactive assistant it's existential. A reactive chatbot can be evaluated in a demo: ask, answer, judge. A proactive assistant can only be evaluated by *living with it* — the value and the annoyance both show up over weeks, in the texture of your actual workday.

Every default in GAIA — what interrupts you, what waits for the briefing, what gets drafted vs. sent — came from us being irritated by our own product until it stopped irritating us. That's the whole methodology.
